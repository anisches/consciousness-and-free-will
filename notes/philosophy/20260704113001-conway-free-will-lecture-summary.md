## Summary
In this lecture, John Conway presents the **Free Will Theorem**, developed jointly with Simon Kochen. The theorem asserts that if human experimenters possess a minimal form of free will—specifically, that their choice of measurement settings is not a function of the past history of the universe—then elementary particles must also possess their own small quantity of free will.

Conway contrasts this with historical philosophical positions:
- **Lucretius**: Anticipated the idea of atomic "swerves" allowing for free will.
- **Descartes**: Proposed a disconnected dualism (mental free will, physical determinism), which the theorem contradicts.
- **Hobbes**: Championed compatibilism, which Conway finds logically consistent but perhaps not descriptive of reality.
- **Leibniz**: His "Principle of Sufficient Reason" is challenged by the indeterminacy of particle behavior.
- **Laplace**: His "Demon" represents the peak of scientific determinism, which the theorem refutes if free will is assumed.

## Core Axioms
1. **SPIN**: The squared spin of a spin-1 particle in three orthogonal directions always yields two 1s and one 0 (the "101 Rule").
2. **TWIN**: Entangled particles ("twins") always give identical answers when measured in the same direction, even when remotely separated.
3. **FIN**: There is a finite limit to the speed at which information can travel (the speed of light), derived from Relativity.

## Key Insights
- **Operational Definition of Free Will**: Conway defines free will simply as a response that is not a function of the past. This avoids baggage regarding consciousness or moral responsibility.
- **Kochen-Specker Paradox**: Proves that particles cannot have pre-determined "hidden variables" (answers to all possible questions) that satisfy the SPIN axiom.
- **Indeterminacy as a Feature**: The lack of total predictability in quantum mechanics is not a temporary defect but a fundamental property of a universe where particles are free.
