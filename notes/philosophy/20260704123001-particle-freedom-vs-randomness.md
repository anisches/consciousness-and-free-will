# Particle Freedom vs. Randomness

John Conway distinguishes between **randomness** and **freedom** (or free will) in the context of the Free Will Theorem.

## The Backgammon Analogy
To illustrate the difference, Conway uses a backgammon tournament analogy:
- In a normal game, players throw dice as they play (randomness during the game).
- In a tournament, a director might provide a pre-determined table of random numbers (e.g., "The first throw is 5 and 3") to all tables.
- From the perspective of the players, the "random" numbers are fixed in advance.
- If a particle's behavior were merely "random," its choices could, in principle, be determined in advance by a "big table of random numbers" in the past.

## Freedom as Non-Predetermination
The Free Will Theorem proves that a particle's behavior is **free** in a sense that is stronger than randomness:
- **Randomness**: Can be determined by a function of the past plus some random input (which itself could be decided in advance).
- **Freedom**: The choice **cannot** be a function of the past history of the universe.

The "randomness" we observe in quantum mechanics (like God playing dice) is often conflated with freedom, but Conway argues that if the choices are non-local and satisfy the SPIN/TWIN axioms, they cannot be pre-recorded or pre-determined even with random variables. Freedom is the property that a choice cannot be made until the experiment is actually performed.

## Philosophical Implications
For philosophers, this distinction is crucial for moral responsibility. If our actions were merely "random" (like a nervous tick), we might not be responsible. However, Conway points out that the opposite of determinism is not randomness, but a new property—freedom—which we do not yet have a formal definition for, other than "not being a function of the past."
