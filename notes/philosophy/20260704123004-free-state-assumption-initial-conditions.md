# The Free State Assumption

In the Free Will Theorem, John Conway and Simon Kochen use the **Free State Assumption** as a more rigorous and less "nebulous" substitute for human free will.

## Replacing Human Free Will
While the theorem is often stated as "If humans have free will, then particles do too," many physicists are uncomfortable with the term "free will." To address this, Conway points out that the only way human free will enters the argument is in the **choice of initial conditions** for an experiment (e.g., which of the 33 directions to measure).

## Definition
The **Free State Assumption** posits that the initial state of an experiment can be essentially **arbitrary**. 
- It is a standard demand of physical theories (since Newton) that they must work for any given initial conditions.
- We do not demand an explanation for why the planets are at a certain spacing; we simply take that as the "given" initial state.
- In the context of the theorem, the "Free State" means that the experimenter's choice of settings is not determined by the past history of the universe.

## Deterministic Free State Theories
The theorem shows that **deterministic Free State theories are impossible.** If you assume that:
1. The universe is deterministic (the future is a function of the past).
2. The initial states (experiment settings) are "free" (not determined by the past).

Then you reach a contradiction with the laws of physics (specifically the SPIN and TWIN axioms). Therefore, if we want to keep our standard scientific practice of assuming free initial states, we must abandon determinism.
