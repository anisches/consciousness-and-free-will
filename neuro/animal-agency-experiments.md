# Animal Experiments Relevant to Agency

Animal experiments do not settle metaphysical free will. Their value is narrower and cleaner: they show mechanisms underneath action selection, voluntary timing, habit, deliberation, and causal control. Use these studies to pressure theories of agency without pretending they answer moral responsibility by themselves.

## 1. Self-Initiated Action Timing

### Murakami, Vicente, Costa, and Mainen - Rats

- Source: Murakami et al., "Neural antecedents of self-initiated actions in secondary motor cortex," _Nature Neuroscience_ (2014). https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3826
- Animal/task: rats performed a waiting task where they internally controlled when to initiate an action.
- Core finding: activity in secondary motor cortex predicted the timing of self-initiated actions before the animal moved.
- Agency relevance: spontaneous-looking action can be preceded by measurable neural dynamics. This is an animal analogue of the readiness-potential problem, but with circuit-level access.
- Caution: this does not show that "the animal had no will." It shows that voluntary timing has neural antecedents.

## 2. Evidence Accumulation and Choice

### Shadlen and Newsome - Macaques

- Source: Shadlen and Newsome, "Neural basis of a perceptual decision in the parietal cortex (area LIP) of the rhesus monkey," _Journal of Neurophysiology_ (2001). https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.2001.86.4.1916
- Animal/task: macaques judged the direction of noisy random-dot motion.
- Core finding: neurons in lateral intraparietal cortex tracked accumulating evidence toward a choice.
- Agency relevance: choice can be modeled as a gradual evidence-accumulation process rather than a single inner moment of decision.
- Caution: perceptual decision-making is not the whole free-will debate. It is strongest for understanding selection under uncertainty.

## 3. Goal-Directed Action Versus Habit

### Dickinson, Balleine, and Outcome Devaluation - Rats

- Sources:
  - Adams and Dickinson, "Instrumental responding following reinforcer devaluation," _Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology_ (1981).
  - Balleine and Dickinson, "Goal-directed instrumental action: contingency and incentive learning and their cortical substrates," _Neuropharmacology_ (1998). https://doi.org/10.1016/S0028-3908(98)00033-1
- Animal/task: rats learned actions for rewards; later the reward was devalued.
- Core finding: goal-directed actions decrease when the outcome loses value, while habits can persist despite devaluation.
- Agency relevance: this gives a practical distinction between flexible agency and automatic behavior.
- Caution: "habit" is not the same as "unfree" in every philosophical sense, but it is less reasons-responsive than goal-directed action.

## 4. Deliberation and Future Simulation

### Johnson, Redish, and Vicarious Trial and Error - Rats

- Sources:
  - Johnson and Redish, "Neural ensembles in CA3 transiently encode paths forward of the animal at a decision point," _Journal of Neuroscience_ (2007). https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.3761-06.2007
  - Redish, "Vicarious trial and error as a fundamental neural mechanism of deliberation and decision-making," _Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience_ (2014). https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbeh.2014.00334
- Animal/task: rats navigated maze choice points.
- Core finding: hippocampal activity represented possible future paths while the animal paused and oriented between alternatives.
- Agency relevance: some animal choices involve prospective simulation, not just reflex or immediate stimulus-response coupling.
- Caution: deliberation here is operational: representing alternatives before acting. It does not require human-style verbal reasoning.

## 5. Causal Manipulation of Action Value and Motor Circuits

### Tai, Wilbrecht, and Striatal Action Value - Rodents

- Source: Tai, Lee, Benavidez, Bonci, and Wilbrecht, "Transient stimulation of distinct subpopulations of striatal neurons mimics changes in action value," _Nature Neuroscience_ (2012). https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3188
- Animal/task: rodents made learned actions while striatal neuron populations were selectively stimulated.
- Core finding: manipulating striatal circuit activity biased action-value expression.
- Agency relevance: choice policy can be causally shifted by changing circuit state.
- Caution: causal manipulability does not automatically erase agency. It shows that agency is implemented through mechanisms that can be perturbed.

### Kravitz and Basal Ganglia Motor Control - Mice

- Source: Kravitz et al., "Regulation of parkinsonian motor behaviours by optogenetic control of basal ganglia circuitry," _Nature_ (2010). https://doi.org/10.1038/nature09159
- Animal/task: mice underwent optogenetic manipulation of direct and indirect basal ganglia pathways.
- Core finding: pathway-specific stimulation altered motor behavior.
- Agency relevance: initiation and suppression of action depend on identifiable control circuits.
- Caution: this is more about motor control than free choice, but it anchors any theory that treats action as disembodied intention.

## Working Order for the Project

1. Goal-directed versus habitual action: best bridge to responsibility and reasons-responsiveness.
2. Evidence accumulation: best bridge to decision formation.
3. Self-initiated action timing: best bridge to readiness-potential debates.
4. Deliberation and future simulation: best bridge to nonverbal prospective agency.
5. Causal manipulation: best bridge to mechanistic intervention and vulnerability.

## Key Takeaway

Animal work gives a layered model of agency:

- action can be prepared before visible movement
- choice can accumulate over time
- actions can be goal-directed or habitual
- some animals simulate alternatives before choosing
- circuit manipulation can bias or trigger behavior

The clean thesis is: animal experiments do not refute free will, but they make naive "uncaused inner chooser" models much harder to defend.
